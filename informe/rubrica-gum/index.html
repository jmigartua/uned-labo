Rúbrica GUM

Los criterios de un buen informe, extraídos del libro de Kirkup y Frenkel (2006); cada uno se comprueba por inspección

Extracted from “An Introduction to Uncertainty in Measurement Using the GUM” (Kirkup & Frenkel, 2006). Deduplicated union of wisdom elements from all 11 chapters. Each criterion is checkable by inspection.

A. Measurand Definition & Experimental Conditions

  1. A1. The measurand is clearly and fully defined, including any conditions (temperature, pressure, material, geometry) that affect its value.
  2. A2. The environmental conditions under which measurements were taken are stated (e.g., ambient temperature, humidity, isolation from disturbances).
  3. A3. The experimental apparatus and setup are described with sufficient detail for reproducibility.

B. Instruments & Calibration

  1. B1. Each instrument used is identified with the magnitude it measures and its resolution (least count).
  2. B2. Calibration status of instruments is stated or referenced; uncalibrated instruments are flagged as introducing potential systematic error.
  3. B3. Traceability of measurements to recognized standards is stated or implied through reference to calibrated instruments.

C. Units & Notation

  1. C1. All measured and calculated values are accompanied by their correct SI units; no quantity is left dimensionless when it should carry a unit.
  2. C2. Values are expressed using appropriate SI prefixes or scientific notation rather than inconveniently large or small raw numbers.
  3. C3. Derived units are correctly formed from base units; unit derivations are shown when non-obvious.

D. Data Collection

  1. D1. Multiple repeated measurements are taken of each quantity (not a single reading), and the number of repetitions n is stated.
  2. D2. Raw measurement data are tabulated explicitly, not only summarized as averages.
  3. D3. Repeatability conditions are described: the report states that repeated measurements were taken under the same (or specified) conditions.

E. Identification of Error Sources

  1. E1. The distinction between random errors and systematic errors is explicitly discussed; sources of each type are identified.
  2. E2. Specific potential sources of systematic error are listed by name (instrument offset, loading effects, parallax, thermal effects, etc.).
  3. E3. Steps taken to detect or minimize systematic errors are stated (calibration checks, lead swapping, using a second instrument, environmental controls).
  4. E4. Known systematic errors are corrected by applying the appropriate correction to measured values, and the correction value and its own uncertainty are stated.
  5. E5. Outlying data points are not silently discarded; any exclusion is explicitly stated and justified.

F. Statistical Analysis of Random Errors (Type A)

  1. F1. The sample mean \bar{x} is reported as the best estimate of the measurand.
  2. F2. The sample standard deviation s is calculated using the (n-1) denominator (unbiased estimator).
  3. F3. The standard uncertainty of the mean is calculated as u(\bar{x}) = s/\sqrt{n} (not s alone), and n is stated.
  4. F4. The number of degrees of freedom \nu = n - 1 associated with the Type A estimate is stated.
  5. F5. Residuals from any fit are calculated, listed or plotted, and examined for systematic patterns (drift, oscillation, trend).
  6. F6. When a visible drift or autocorrelation is detected in repeated readings, s/\sqrt{n} is not blindly applied; a conservative estimate or trend correction is used instead.

G. Type B Uncertainty Evaluation

  1. G1. Type B uncertainties are evaluated from calibration reports, manufacturer specifications, reference data, or physical reasoning — and the source is cited for each.
  2. G2. For instrument resolution (digital): a rectangular distribution of half-width a = half the least significant digit is assumed, yielding u = a/\sqrt{3}.
  3. G3. For instrument resolution (analog scale): u = w/\sqrt{12} where w is the full graduation width.
  4. G4. The assumed probability distribution (rectangular, Gaussian, triangular) for each Type B input is stated or implied.
  5. G5. The degrees of freedom for each Type B component is estimated via \nu = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{u(u)}{u}\right)^{-2} when the proportional uncertainty in the uncertainty is known.

H. Uncertainty Propagation

  1. H1. The measurand model y = f(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n) is written down explicitly, identifying all input quantities.
  2. H2. The sensitivity coefficient c_i = \partial y / \partial x_i is calculated and stated for each input.
  3. H3. The combined standard uncertainty is computed by the law of propagation (RSS): u_c^2(y) = \sum c_i^2 u^2(x_i).
  4. H4. Uncertainties are not summed linearly (worst-case) when inputs are uncorrelated; the RSS rule is applied.
  5. H5. Individual uncertainty contributions |c_i \cdot u(x_i)| are tabulated, making clear which inputs dominate the combined uncertainty.
  6. H6. When inputs are correlated (e.g., measured with the same instrument), the covariance term 2r \cdot c_i c_j u(x_i) u(x_j) is included in the propagation.
  7. H7. Dimensional consistency of all terms in the propagation expression is verified.
  8. H8. Type A and Type B components are combined by RSS (not kept separate through the final answer).

I. Least-Squares Fitting

  1. I1. When a linear relationship is expected, least-squares fitting is applied to estimate slope and intercept.
  2. I2. The standard uncertainties of the fitted slope u(b) and intercept u(a) are calculated and reported.
  3. I3. The rms residual s is calculated and reported as a measure of goodness of fit.
  4. I4. Degrees of freedom for the fit residual variance is stated as \nu = n - p (where p is the number of parameters).
  5. I5. The linear correlation coefficient r is calculated and reported when a linear regression is performed.
  6. I6. The data and fitted line are plotted on the same graph (predictor on x-axis, response on y-axis).
  7. I7. When a power law is used, the equation is linearized (e.g., via logarithm) before fitting, and the linearization step is shown.

J. Expanded Uncertainty & Coverage Interval

  1. J1. The Welch-Satterthwaite formula is applied to determine the effective degrees of freedom \nu_{\text{eff}} from individual \nu_i and uncertainty contributions.
  2. J2. \nu_{\text{eff}} is truncated (not rounded) to the nearest integer before looking up the coverage factor.
  3. J3. The coverage factor k is obtained from the t-distribution table at \nu_{\text{eff}} and the chosen confidence level (not assumed to be 1.96 unless \nu is very large).
  4. J4. The chosen confidence level (e.g., 95%) is explicitly stated.
  5. J5. The expanded uncertainty U = k \cdot u_c is reported to no more than two significant figures.
  6. J6. The final result is stated as: \text{measurand} = \text{value} \pm U (unit), with k and confidence level stated.

K. Significant Figures & Rounding

  1. K1. All reported numbers are rounded to a justifiable number of significant figures reflecting the precision of the measurement.
  2. K2. Intermediate calculations retain full precision; rounding is applied only to the final reported value.
  3. K3. The number of significant figures is not greater than what the measurement uncertainty supports.
  4. K4. Proportional (relative) uncertainties are stated when comparing the accuracy of different measurements.

L. Graphs & Visual Presentation

  1. L1. A graph of the raw data with the fitted model is included.
  2. L2. A residuals plot is produced and inspected for systematic trends.
  3. L3. When the sample size is sufficient (n \geq 20), a histogram of repeated measurements is presented to assess distribution shape.

M. Discussion & Comparison

  1. M1. The experimental result is compared to a published reference or theoretical value, with the comparison stated explicitly.
  2. M2. When two or more methods yield discrepant results, the discrepancy is identified and an explanation attempted.
  3. M3. The adequacy of the measurement uncertainty is assessed: is it fit for the intended purpose?
  4. M4. The dominant sources of uncertainty are identified and suggestions for reducing them are offered.

N. Uncertainty Budget Table

  1. N1. An uncertainty budget table is included that lists each input quantity, its best estimate, its standard uncertainty, its type (A or B), its sensitivity coefficient c_i, the contribution |c_i \cdot u(x_i)|, and the degrees of freedom \nu_i.

Total: 61 unique checkable criteria organized in 14 categories (A-N).
